Verbal principles

How we speak.

PrincipleRulesExample
01 We speak with conviction and openness“Our read is” before “this is how it is.” Every claim has its why and its evidence. Confident, never arrogant.“This is what we’re seeing. Let’s figure out what to do with it.”
02 We ask questions that open things upAsk for the client’s perspective before giving ours. Adjust our assumptions to what we hear.“Before we tell you how we see it, how does this play out for you day to day?”
03 We say what needs saying, with careGive the conclusion as it is, with respect and the why. Offer an alternative before ruling out what’s there.“We think there’s a better option. Let us show you which one and why.”
04 We speak plainly to make things happenShort, concrete sentences. Action verbs: integrate, support, build, measure. Explain technical terms. “Usage” before “adoption.”“It’s built. Now let’s get it used.”
05 We speak as a partner“We” when the problem is shared. We work side by side. Firm and close at the same time.“Let’s think it through together and find a way to make it happen.”
06 We speak from what we’ve builtReal examples before concepts. Show a prototype, demo, or data point first. Share what worked and what didn’t.“We’ve already built this. Let us show you how it works.”
07 We talk about AI naturallyProblem and outcome first, then the tool. Say what it solves, with what data, and what it can’t do yet. Never to impress.“We use AI to process field reports, so the agronomist gets the recommendation in minutes.”

Writing in English · English-only rules

Write natively, never translate.

Lead with the point.

US executives read two lines and decide. Conclusion, ask, or finding first.

Use vs. usage.

“Usage” names the territory, metric, and loop. “Use” is the verb and plain-sentence word.

Active voice and “we.”

“We built the integration.” Passive voice makes us sound absent.

Client, customer, user.

Our companies are clients; their buyers are customers; people in their products are users.

Contractions on LinkedIn, the website, and email.

Fewer in proposals and SOWs.

No “the space” for the sector.

Say agriculture, ag, or the industry. “Space” is for our three spaces.

One qualifier is enough.

“Our read is” carries conviction with openness. Don’t stack softeners.

No Spanish carry-overs:

“not X, but Y,” “an agriculture where,” stacked “with” clauses, em dashes.

Serial comma, always.

“Strategy, design and development, and adoption and usage.”

Plain words.

Use, not leverage. Clear, not seamless. No cutting-edge, world-class, robust, unlock, empower, holistic.

US spelling and formats (mandatory for US content):

organization, analyze; October 21, 2026; 36%; $2M.

Sentence-case headings.

Never Title Case.

How Magoya sounds

How Magoya sounds:
not this, this.

TopicNot thisThisWhy

How Magoya sounds · LinkedIn

A post that sounds like us.

LinkedIn · Spokesperson: Cami · Territory: Usage

Onboarding for a software tool starts long before launch. It starts in design: what users see first, how they understand the first flow, and when they can keep going on their own.

When a tool has already launched and usage isn’t taking off, we work on three fronts: an onboarding, adoption, and loyalty journey run by customer success, virtual and in the field; AI agents that scale that support so it’s there exactly when the user needs it; and feedback loops that take what we learn straight back into the product.

With one client, we went from 12% to 36% usage. Field support showed us where people got stuck, those lessons went back into the design, and today the product solves them on its own.

The goal is a product that scales without depending on more people. Every conversation in the field is an improvement the next user won’t need.

Before publishingThe 12% to 36% figure needs team validation.

Why it works

  • Curious: finds where users get stuck before assuming the cause.
  • No “help” or “technology.”
  • Adoption appears as part of usage.
  • Usage is designed from the start.
  • AI is one front, in service of the user.
  • A concrete figure and an explicit three-front method.
